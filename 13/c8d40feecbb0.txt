Introduction
Delhi has become uninhabitable for the better part of
the year. Its summer is unbearably hot and its winter
particularly unbreathable.
A study by the Centre for Science and Environment
(CSE), which analysed Landsat 8 satellite images, reported that the land surface temperature (LST) have
soared in different parts of Delhi between 2014-2022
(see figure 1). While there were some relatively blue
spots, which signifies cooler temperatures in the figure, in 2014, it is mostly deep red 8 years later. As
per this study, Delhi’s highest ever LST of 53.9°C was
recorded on May 16, 2020. To be sure, the LST is
usually higher than the air temperature, but even the
latter has touched a record 52.3°C on 29 May 2024
during the heatwave that year, according to the India
Meteorological Department (IMD).
While the winter months give respite from the sweltering heat of May, they come with their own set of

problems. Pollution is at its peak during the months
of Oct-Jan every year. We must add an important
caveat here. Talking about pollution for the winters
in Delhi gives the impression as if it is under control
rest of the year. Nothing could be further from the
truth. Pollution in Delhi is a yearlong problem. It
just gets dangerously worse during the winters. Figure
2, based on Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB)
data, shows how the quality of air has been over a period of 7 years (2017-2023). A few things stand out in
the figure. One, Delhi has had only two days of healthy
air per year (as per the WHO standards of AQI<50)!
Two, people are inhaling air unfit for breathing for
close to five months every year on an average. Three,
and quite remarkably, even for 2020, a lockdown year,
things have only marginally been better (5 instead of
2 days of healthy air quality). It’s clear there is something systemically wrong.
Part of Delhi’s problem is global, and part local.
Soaring temperatures over time is reflective of climate
change for which Delhi alone is not responsible. But a
significant part of the rise in the temperature is contributed by the city and its surrounding urban areas. Delhi, like other urban centres world over, suffers from what is called the Urban Heat Island (UHI)
effect, where urban metropolitan areas experience significantly higher temperatures than their surrounding

cooled the air down; tall buildings trapping heat and

is a feedback loop here. The hotter a city becomes,
more is the use of air cooling devices, which further

Pollution, on the other hand, is much more of a
local problem. The sources of pollution are local since
pollutants have a geographically concentrated impact.
The sources of pollution in Delhi come primarily from
the way Delhi travels, the way it builds its offices and
homes, and the stubble burning that takes place in the
nearby rural areas during the winter season. Some of
these polluting sources can be eliminated while others
can be controlled to a large extent.
Whatever the causes, the impacts of heat or pollution are not class neutral. The poor suffer more from
both heat stress and pollution because of where and
how they have to live as well as a lack of financial
means to adapt to these changes.
Against this backdrop, this study looks at the rising heat stress and air pollution in Delhi. The idea
is to understand what are people’s perceptions about

and how are they coping with the twin crises. To what
extent is their class position shielding them from the ill
effects of these crises? Do they believe the government
is doing enough to tackle them? And last, but not the
least, what, in their opinion, can be done about it?
This report has the following structure. Chapter 1
presents a thematic summary of literature on these issues both at the global level and Delhi specific studies.
Chapter 2 describes the survey design. Our survey was
visualised at two levels – home and workplace. Heat

been explored in chapter 3, which are responses at the

4, the information for which has been collected for all
working adults in these households. The questionnaire
used has been attached at the end of this report.

General Studies on Heat Stress
and Climate Vulnerability
Global and national research underscores that heatrelated mortality risks rise non-linearly, with significant vulnerability among the elderly, children under
five, and those with pre-existing conditions Prayas
(Health Group) [2022]. In India, excess mortality
during heatwaves has been documented as high as
43.1% in cities like Ahmedabad, often resulting in severe acute morbidity and multiple organ dysfunction
Mourougan et al. [2024]. To manage these risks, inter-

national standards like ISO 7243 provide a framework
for assessing occupational heat stress using the Wet
Bulb Globe Temperature (WBGT) index across various work intensities ISO [2017], ?], Venugopal et al.
[2015]. Successful interventions, such as the Ahmedabad Heat Action Plan, demonstrate that coordinated
early warnings and community adaptation can effectively reduce these death tolls Ahmedabad Municipal
Corporation [2018].
The economic burden of heat is equally severe,
with India projected to lose 5.8% of its total working
hours—the equivalent of 34 million jobs—by 2030 due
to thermal stress ILO [2019]. Micro-level data from
Indian manufacturing shows that a 1°C temperature
rise correlates with an average 2.1% drop in annual
output, primarily through reduced labor productivity
and increased absenteeism even in climate-controlled
environments Somanathan et al. [2021]. This loss of
work capacity is particularly acute in tropical regions,
where projected global GDP losses could exceed 20%
by the century’s end Kjellstrom et al. [2016].
Vulnerability is often structural, as the “assetless poor” are trapped in cycles where environmental degradation forces them into low-paid, hazardous
work to meet basic subsistence needs Barbier [2010].
Social protection measures like MGNREGS can mitigate this precarity, with access to job cards reduc5

higher concentrations of PM2.5 and NO2 Hajat et al.

Delhi Studies on Heat Stress and
Informal Labor
In Delhi, nearly 88% of the city is categorized as being
under moderate to highest heat stress risk, with spatial hazards heavily influenced by population density
and weak infrastructure Kacker et al. [2024]. Although
summer air temperatures have remained relatively stable, an 8% rise in humidity has added 3.3°C of effective heat stress to the urban environment via the Heat
Index CSE [2024]. The urban heat island effect prevents nighttime cooling in the city core, while rapid
urbanization and built-up expansion correlate directly
with rising land surface temperatures that frequently

exceed 40°C CSE [2024], Das et al. [2024]. These
factors collectively challenge Delhi’s progress toward
global sustainability goals Kumar et al. [2021b], Debnath et al. [2023].
The health consequences are stark, as landlocked
Delhi records up to 43.4% higher mortality during extreme heat events compared to coastal metros Bont
et al. [2024]. Current monitoring often under-reports
this risk; personalized indices show that nearly 98%
of the city can be under extreme heat stress during

Informal workers bear the heaviest burden, with
heatwaves causing net earnings to plummet by 40%
and every 1°C increase in wet-bulb temperature reducing labor supply by 19% Patwary [2025]. Workers in Delhi’s slums report reshaping their workdays
to night shifts to avoid peak heat, leading to chronic

impairment from the dual exposure to extreme heat
and hazardous air Barthwal et al. [2022].

distribution of resources; wards with high proportions
of children and outdoor laborers often have the least
access to mitigating greenspace Mitchell et al. [2021].
The 2024 Delhi Heat Action Plan attempts to address this through multi-sectoral coordination and infrastructure upgrades like cool roofs and water kiosks
DDMA [2024]. However, public support for such policies can be polarized by gender and elite conflict,
particularly regarding exemptions for specific groups
from environmental regulations Beiser-McGrath et al.
[2021].

Survey Design
How were the samples drawn?
Since the study requires a coverage of residents from
different income brackets, we chose six localities in
Delhi – Adarsh Nagar middle income group (MIG),
Adarsh Nagar jhuggi jhopri (JJ) Cluster, New Seemapuri JJ Cluster, Tughlakabad extension, Hauz Khas,
Vasant Kunj (see figure 2.1). The idea was to gather
samples from the low income group, the middle income group, and the high income group households in
the city. Of these six location, two each belong to the
three broad income profiles. Our survey shows that
there is no clear one to one correspondence between the
two locations and their income profiles. While the JJ

clusters, by and large, covered the low income groups,
some low income groups (especially tenants of smaller
houses in the MIG areas) were living in the MIG areas
as well. Such an overlap did not exist in the affluent
part of the city.
While the households in the Hauz Khas and Vasant Kunj areas were uniquely identifiable with their
apartment/house number, the same was not the case
for the remaining four areas. So, we had to use two
different ways to draw random samples. For the high
income houses, we fed all the unique apartment/house
numbers to a computer programme and asked it to
randomly identify a certain number of houses which
the teams could survey. For the rest of the households,
we fed the google coordinates of the outer bound of the
area to be surveyed and asked Python to generate geographical coordinates (shown as blue pin drops in the
figure) for a certain number of households depending
on the total number of households we had to survey in
that particular locality.
There was an additional issue to take care of for
the MIG localities because, unlike the mostly single
storeyed JJ cluster houses, these were 2/3 storeyed
buildings. Since the extent of heat stress varies according to the floor one lives on (with top floor being
the hottest), we had to also randomise the floor for
this set of households. Apart from the google coordi9

The Survey Process
There were 7 teams conducting the survey over a period of nine days between September 14-22, 2025. The
month of September is neither very hot nor is it as polluted as the winter months, so it can be safely assumed
that the responses are not biased by the timing of the
survey. Moreover the questionnaire was designed in a
way which required people to respond with a longer
time horizon in mind instead of an immediate recall
period. The survey was conducted using digital devices with the help of the kobotool app.
The total sample collected was of 582 households
with 2022 adults (defined as those above the age of
15 years).
The survey team consisted of 18 people. 13 of these
were carefully selected through a rigorous process of interviews based on their knowledge of the subject and
familiarity with the survey process. These 13 were ac-

companied by a lead surveyor who also provided local
support in the JJ clusters and MIG areas, one research
assistant associated with the project, a PhD scholar
working in the field of climate change who volunteered
for the work, the co-PI, and a volunteer from Japan,
Keisuke Izume, who took photographs of the survey
process, a collage of some is shown in figure 2.2. The
selected surveyors, the lead surveyor, and the research
assistant were paid commensurately for their services.
However, no monetary incentives were given to the respondents for participating in the survey.

Health
Rising pollution and even hotter weather is going to
aggravate health related problems, so we turn to that
next.
When it comes to chronic illness, its incidence is
similar across classes (see figure 3.8). The word cloud
throws some light on the nature of such illness of those
who have it. While the rich have lifestyle health problems like diabetes, sugar, they also have pollution related health conditions, which just shows how pollution affects, albeit unequally, all the classes.
How do people deal with such chronic illness?
While India adopted a mixed model of public and private healthcare since its independence, things have
tilted in favour of private healthcare over time, especially since the economic reforms of the 1990s. In
terms of the nature of government intervention, the
earlier model was one of direct public health provisioning with government hospitals/dispensaries as well as
central and state health schemes which insulated particularly the poor and the vulnerable as well as government employees in particular.
But since the economic reforms, the state has gradually withdrawn from this model of direct health care

provisioning either through starving government hospitals of funds or withdrawing health schemes1 which
insulated the poor against health related problems.
While the change started from the 1990s, it has
gained momentum during the current regime, where
India has moved to a more insurance based health
model, much like what’s prevalent in countries such
as the US. A pan-India health insurance scheme called
Ayushman Bharat Yojana (crudely translated as ’Long
live India programme’) covers approximately 50 crore
rural/urban poor. It has recently been expanded to
include all senior citizens across classes.
There is an inherent class bias in access to private
hospitals, so the slow death of public hospitals make
the poor especially vulnerable. The biggest casualty
of this insurance-hospital healtcare model is steeply
rising costs of healthcare, especially when it comes to
inpatient treatments (i.e. those which require hospitalisation). It is said that a typical Indian family with
even a middle income status is just one hospitalisation away from poverty. In such a situation, access to
health insurance is a matter of life and death in the
case of poor. The top chart in figure 3.8, which depicts
access to health insurance across the three classes,
gains special significance when seen in this backdrop.

Perception and Impact
Classwise Perception
When asked the question if there has been a general
rise in temperatures in the recent years in comparison to a decade or two earlier, most people, across the
three classes, believe it has. Of these, a small share
believe it has increased marginally whereas a significant majority believed it has increased a lot. There is
a slight variation across classes in those who believe
the latter. While only one of five households among
the rich and the middle classes believe it has not increased by a lot, for the poor it is every third house-

hold. Though we cannot say with certainty why this
