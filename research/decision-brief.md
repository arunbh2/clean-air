# Shared air, unequal costs

### Where philanthropy can improve health and livelihood security in India
**Decision brief · Research cutoff 22 November 2026**

## Core judgement

India's air pollution is a mass health problem with an unequal economic edge. The air is shared, but the costs are not.

People who work outdoors, live in informal housing, or depend on daily earnings breathe the most and can avoid the least [C031][C046][C082]. In Delhi-NCR they also lose income when emergency rules stop work, and the compensation the law promises reaches registered workers far more often than migrants [C038][C041][C044].

The strongest opportunities for a foundation are therefore not new pieces of clean-air hardware. They are three things:

1. making existing protections reach the people they were written for;
2. backing a proven, low-cost school programme;
3. paying for independent evidence before public money is spent on visible but unproven measures.

## Why this matters for vulnerable communities

- **The burden is large and Indian evidence now confirms it.** GBD 2019 attributes 1.67 million deaths in 2019 to air pollution [C016]. Studies using Indian death records find mortality rising with PM2.5 even below the national daily limit [C053][C019].
- **The standard is not safety.** India's annual limit for fine particles is eight times WHO's guideline [C004].
- **The AQI hides the worst hours.** It is capped at 500, and readings above 999 µg/m³ are excluded from it [C008][C010].
- **Most of India is unmeasured.** About 40% of districts have no government monitor [C033]. Rural Bihar breaches the national standard on nearly nine days in ten [C058]. An unmonitored district is not a safe one.
- **Exposure follows work.** Sanitation workers, waste pickers and guards in Delhi show abnormal lung function at far higher rates than office workers [C082]. Delivery riders breathe several times the ambient level [C099].

## The most consequential livelihood pathways

| Pathway | Evidence | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **Pollution-control restrictions** stop work and wages | **Strong** | Court-ordered compensation exists; registration gaps leave most construction workers out [C039][C041][C042] |
| **Illness** reduces capacity and adds medical costs | Health effect **Strong**; route to earnings **unmeasured** | Medical spending rises in pollution months (self-reported) [C047] |
| **Avoidance** costs money | **Limited/mixed** | Air purifiers and air conditioning are concentrated among the affluent [C046] |
| **Transition costs** fall on workers | **No adequate evidence found** on outcomes | Kiln conversion cuts emissions; workers' pay and debt unmeasured [C069][C071] |
| **School closures** disrupt learning and care | **Limited/mixed** | Up to 15 days of closures; unequal digital access [C054][C055] |

National GDP-loss estimates (US$36.8 billion in 2019 [C018]) describe the economy. They do not describe a household's finances and are not a measure of household finances.

## Geographic differences

**Delhi-NCR** shows how emergency control creates its own livelihood crisis [C040]. **Bihar** shows exposure that official networks barely see, with household fuel as both source and exposure [C058][C061]. **Singrauli** shows industrial exceedances with little health surveillance [C062][C063]. **Mumbai** shows emission inventories missing informal sources [C066]. The **paddy belt** shows burning harming farming villages themselves [C068]. **Kiln clusters** show cleaner technology arriving without labour protection [C069][C071].

None of these settings stands in for another.

## Priorities

This dossier prioritises two groups:

1. **Outdoor informal workers in Delhi-NCR**: construction workers, sanitation workers, waste pickers, guards and riders.
2. **Children in high-pollution cities.**

Both combine strong evidence of harm with institutions that can carry the work and outcomes visible within a season or school year [C082][C044][C074]. Kiln clusters and rural household energy stay in the learning agenda.

## Strongest opportunities

**Fund now (class A)**

- **Enrol waste pickers and sanitation workers in what already exists.** That means NAMASTE profiling, PPE and health insurance (open to waste pickers since 2024–25), municipal ID cards that cities have been slow to issue, and the annual health check-ups the OSH Code gives employees [C106][C107][C109]. *Operator:* worker organisations, with municipalities and the scheme as providers.
- **Close the compensation gap.** Fund registration and renewal drives, payment tracking after each GRAP closure, legal support, and independent reporting on who is missed. Public funds pay the transfers: 44% of construction-welfare cess was unspent nationally [C101]. *Operator:* worker organisations, with the welfare board as payer.
- **School air-pollution education.** In a two-year randomised trial across 180 Delhi classes, it improved protective behaviour and reduced reported breathing difficulty [C074]. *Operator:* school systems.
- **Independent evaluation of public clean-air spending.** The smog-tower evaluation showed what evidence can stop [C077]. Unproven measures are still expanding [C081].

**Test first (classes B–C)**

- income continuity for vendors and platform workers through the new social security fund [C103];
- classroom purifiers, redesigned to avoid the behavioural offset seen in the trial [C075];
- occupational respirator programmes for sanitation workers, waste pickers and riders, with employer co-payment;
- sensor networks with calibration and written response protocols;
- filtration in existing night shelters and childcare centres;
- kiln conversion tied to labour standards.

## Infrastructure judgement

Infrastructure earns its place when four conditions hold:

- it targets a specific pollutant;
- the intended users spend real time in it;
- an existing institution runs and pays for it;
- its effect is measured.

**Smog towers fail this test.** Their effect fades within about 200 m, at about ₹25 crore to build and ₹15 lakh a month to run [C077][C078]. Anti-smog guns lack evidence [C080]. Purpose-built clean-air shelters ask outdoor workers to give up earnings to use them [C086][C098].

Classroom purifiers clean the air, but in the Delhi trial children's outcomes lagged those of education alone [C075]. The most credible infrastructure is filtration and monitoring added to services that already exist, tested before it scales.

## Do not fund on current evidence

- smog towers;
- anti-smog guns and sprinklers as air-quality measures;
- untargeted mask distribution;
- purpose-built clean-air shelters;
- crop-residue machinery as a philanthropic purchase;
- statutory transfers or fuel subsidies paid on behalf of public duty-bearers.

## Evidence gaps that matter most

1. Informal workers' earnings losses during episodes, with illness, restrictions and demand separated (G1).
2. Whether registration support increases compensation received by migrants (G2).
3. What purifiers achieve once behavioural offsets are addressed (G3).
4. Whether respirator programmes cut outdoor workers' exposure in practice (G4).
5. What happens to kiln workers after conversion (G6).

## Decisions for a foundation

1. **Protection of income, or protection of lungs, or both?** The evidence is strongest that restriction-driven income loss is avoidable and that existing law can address it. Exposure protection for workers is needed but less proven.
2. **Build new things, or make existing institutions deliver?** Welfare boards, school systems, shelter agencies and health departments already carry legal duties. Grants that strengthen their delivery can outlast themselves. Grants that create parallel assets usually cannot.
3. **How fast to move beyond Delhi-NCR?** The priority groups are anchored where duties, data and partners exist. The same groups live in Patna, Lucknow and Mumbai, and the largest unseen burden lies in under-monitored districts. A second city can be chosen once the first season's results show what transfers.
