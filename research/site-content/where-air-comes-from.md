## Five steps from a source to a person

1. **A source emits.** A stove, truck, kiln, power plant, field fire or rubbish fire releases particles and gases.
2. **Pollution travels or forms in the air.** Gases such as sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides and ammonia react to form new particles, which can travel hundreds of kilometres [C237].
3. **Weather and land shape concentrations.** Flat plains ringed by mountains and winter temperature inversions trap pollution; concentrations can rise without any change in emissions [C223].
4. **People breathe different amounts.** Exposure differs at home, at school, in transit and at work; people in traffic breathe more combustion particles than nearby monitors show [C031].
5. **Governments control different parts of the airshed.** No city's boundary contains the whole problem [C225].

## Two different questions

The World Bank's model answers two questions that are easy to confuse. **Where did this pollution originate?** (local, other states or provinces, other countries, natural) and **Which activities produced it?** (cooking, industry, transport, agriculture, waste, fires). The shares answer different questions and cannot be added together or put in one chart.

The book's charts show bars without printed values, so this page reproduces only figures stated in its text and marks the rest as not printed. Some jurisdiction figures in Chapter 2 differ from the overview and the chart [C232]; both are shown.

## What this does not tell us

- It does not give Delhi's source mix: the book's 33% household-cooking figure is a regional average, and Delhi's own source studies give different results by season and method [C025] [C029].
- It does not measure personal exposure, which can be much higher near traffic [C032].
- It does not cover Southeast Asia, whose cities have different source mixes [C133].
- A source's share in a modelled average does not make it the main source at every hotspot.
