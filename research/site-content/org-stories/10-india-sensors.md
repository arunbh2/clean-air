## The problem in everyday language

Low-cost sensors are cheap enough to put on every street, school and building site. If their readings are wrong, warnings, fines and plans built on them are wrong too.

## The actors and their different roles

CSTEP, a Bengaluru think tank, runs an independent test facility [C201]. Respirer, Airveda and Aurassure are Indian companies whose devices were tested [C202]. Oizom is another company that reports large smart-city deployments [C198]. Airparif, a French air agency, ran similar sensor challenges in Thailand and India with Clean Air Fund support [C169].

## What they did

CSTEP set up its facility in December 2023 [C201]. In April 2026 it published results for 48 devices from six manufacturers, placed next to a reference monitor for up to eight months [C202]. Some companies sell into compliance markets: Airveda says it is an approved vendor for construction-site monitoring in Delhi and Maharashtra [C199].

## Who paid

The facility's funding is not stated in the sources read. Respirer's earlier 10-city network was funded by Shakti Sustainable Energy Foundation [C197].

## What evidence of change exists

An independent finding: none of the manufacturers met precision targets for nitrogen dioxide and ozone [C202]. This is evidence about measurement quality, not about cleaner air.

## What remains uncertain

How individual devices performed for PM2.5. Whether regulators require independent testing before accepting sensor data.

## What another place could learn

Independent testing is cheap compared with the cost of decisions made on bad data, and is a natural role for philanthropy.
