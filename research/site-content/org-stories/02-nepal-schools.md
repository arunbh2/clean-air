## The problem in everyday language

Parents in Kathmandu have had no easy way to know how polluted the air is at their child's school, and many Nepali towns have no public monitor that people can check.

## The actors and their different roles

Clean Up Nepal led a schools project with Green Decision Labs, a Nepali research company, as consortium partner [C185] [C175]. Clean Energy Nepal, a national NGO, sets up city monitors with municipalities [C186] [C174]. The EPIC Air Quality Fund pays for both [C154]. OpenAQ hosts the open data [C185].

## What they did

Clean Up Nepal reports 40 PM2.5 sensors in Kathmandu schools, public data and awareness sessions for 1,500 students and 80 teachers [C185]. Green Decision Labs' award aims to find which schools are worst affected [C175]. Clean Energy Nepal is installing 21 monitors in major cities and training young people to share the data [C174], with a Nepali-language dashboard [C186].

## Who paid

EPIC Air Quality Fund grants, launched with a US$1.5 million gift from Coefficient Giving [C154]. Individual grant amounts are not published.

## What evidence of change exists

Sensors were installed and sessions held, as reported by the organisations. No source records lower exposure in classrooms, fewer illnesses or a school or municipal decision taken because of the data.

## What remains uncertain

Who will maintain and calibrate the sensors after the grant. Whether schools will change anything they can control, such as timetables on bad-air days or filtration. The schools project is listed as completed.

## What another place could learn

Low-cost school monitoring can make children's exposure visible quickly. It needs a named decision-maker, a calibration plan and a budget for action to become protection.
