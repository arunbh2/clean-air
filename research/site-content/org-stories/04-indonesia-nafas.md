## The problem in everyday language

People in Jakarta want to know what they are breathing on their own street, not just at a few official stations.

## The actors and their different roles

Nafas Indonesia is a company that runs outdoor sensors and an app [C204]. Nafas Foundation is a non-profit initiative set up by the company, which now presents the public data and research [C205]. Airly, a European company, supplies the sensors [C204]. DBS Foundation, the philanthropic arm of a bank, gave Nafas a grant [C155]. The University of Indonesia's Faculty of Public Health co-wrote a health study [C207].

## What they did

The company says it runs more than 100 sensors in Greater Jakarta [C204]; the foundation says the network now has more than 219 sensors in 23 cities [C205]. The DBS grant paid for 50 new sensors; 19 were installed by September 2024 [C155] [C206]. A 2025 white paper reported that higher PM2.5 was associated with more pneumonia among Jakarta's under-fives [C207].

## Who paid

A DBS Foundation Business for Impact grant (amount not published) [C155]. Sensor data are also sponsored by companies, according to Nafas's own page [C204].

## What evidence of change exists

The network's size is company-reported. No independent audit of data quality was found. The pneumonia finding is an association in a white paper, not a causal estimate [C207].

## What remains uncertain

Who is accountable for the data: the company or the foundation. How sponsorship affects where sensors go.

## What another place could learn

Private networks can fill gaps quickly. Public trust needs clear separation of commercial and non-profit roles, open methods and independent checks.
