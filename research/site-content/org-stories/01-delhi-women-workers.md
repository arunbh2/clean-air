## The problem in everyday language

Women who carry bricks and mix cement on Delhi's building sites breathe dust and smoke all day, then go home to settlements where air is rarely measured. When the city halts construction during smog, work stops, and only registered workers are eligible for compensation [C040].

## The actors and their different roles

Mahila Housing Trust, a women's housing organisation, and Help Delhi Breathe, a citizen campaign, run the training [C195]. Clean Air Fund pays for it [C156] [C088]. When construction is halted, Delhi has announced compensation for workers registered with the Delhi Building and Other Construction Workers Welfare Board, the public body that pays it [C040] [C043]. A union affidavit says some workers still await payment [C043].

## What they did

Women from unrecognised settlements are trained as "AQI ambassadors". They carry portable monitors, explain readings to neighbours and help people reach existing government protection [C195] [C156]. Clean Air Fund reports more than 75 women construction workers trained in three resettlement colonies [C088].

## Who paid

Clean Air Fund grants in 2021 and 2023; amounts are not published [C156]. Compensation, where it is paid, comes through the welfare board, not from the grants [C040] [C043].

## What evidence of change exists

Activities and outputs are documented. No source measures whether the women's exposure, health or income changed.

## What remains uncertain

How many women were helped to register and paid. Whether information without paid time off can change exposure. Clean Air Fund's survey found that most women avoid raising air pollution at work for fear of losing their jobs [C089].

## What another place could learn

Pairing outreach with a public duty that already exists, here a welfare board with money, gives training somewhere to lead. Whether this works elsewhere depends on whether such a fund exists and pays.
