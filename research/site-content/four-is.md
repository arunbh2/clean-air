The World Bank calls these the 4Is [C239]. They are prerequisites for change, not a score and not proof that a measure works."
limit: "The 4Is describe what implementation needs. They do not show that any programme succeeded; outcomes still need measuring."

## Why this matters

Over the past decade, cleaner cooking, local industrial retrofits, better fuels and cleaner vehicles have not translated into significantly cleaner air across the Indo-Gangetic plain [C230]. The World Bank's reading is that the region's challenge lies less in knowing what to do than in delivering it [C261]. Each of the four prerequisites below can be the reason a sound technology fails.

## Worked example 1: a cleaner stove is not the same as cleaner cooking

| Step | What has to be true | What kind of step it is |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Purchase or connection | The household gets a gas, electric or improved stove | Output, easy to count |
| 2. Fuel available | Refills or power arrive reliably, including in rural areas | Condition (infrastructure) |
| 3. Affordable to keep using | Refills fit the household budget month after month | Condition (incentives) |
| 4. Old fuel set aside | The household stops using wood or dung alongside the new stove (fuel stacking) | Behaviour, rarely measured |
| 5. Sustained use | Clean cooking continues after the programme ends | Measured outcome, sometimes |
| 6. Lower exposure and better health | Personal exposure falls and health improves | Research question |

In India, connections under the Ujjwala scheme rose fast, but in 2021-22 more than half of beneficiaries bought four or fewer refills against about seven needed for exclusive use [C036]. The World Bank lists refill costs, supply gaps, taste preferences, women's time and fuel stacking as barriers [C241], and proposes pay-as-you-go finance, women-led delivery and results-based finance [C242]. In the HAPIN trial, near-complete use of free gas stoves cut exposure but did not change birth weight [C096], a reminder that step 6 is not automatic.

## Worked example 2: a small brick kiln

Converting a fixed-chimney kiln to a zigzag design cuts emissions sharply and pays back in a few years on paper [C244]. Yet adoption stays limited [C245]:

- **Incentives:** an upfront cost of tens of thousands of dollars for owners on thin margins; the World Bank proposes credit lines, partial guarantees and capital subsidies [C246].
- **Information:** owners lack data on schemes, suppliers and performance; satellite mapping can target enforcement.
- **Institutions:** standards exist but enforcement is weak, and environment and industry ministries often work in silos.
- **Infrastructure and skills:** converted kilns need trained workers to reach their efficiency [C070].

What happens to kiln workers' pay, debt and exposure after conversion has not been measured [C071].

## Worked example 3: crop residue

A ban on burning does not by itself give a smallholder a machine, a buyer for straw or more days between harvest and sowing. About 540 million people in the region live on farms of two to three hectares, and the World Bank finds mechanised residue management unaffordable for most of them [C263]. Fires in Punjab fell sharply in 2025, which the commission credits to machinery, enforcement and ex-situ use together [C067].
