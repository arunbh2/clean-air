Conduct an evidence-grounded study of air pollution, livelihoods and
unequal protection in India.

Working title:
“Shared air, unequal costs: where philanthropy can improve health
and livelihood security in India”

DELIVERABLE AND AUDIENCE

Produce a completed research dossier and a concise decision brief.

Write for a senior foundation decision-maker considering whether and
how to develop a grantmaking portfolio in this field. Keep that audience
implicit in the prose: use clear judgement, credible evidence and
practical choices, without repeatedly addressing “the board” or
promoting a predetermined programme.

The research should establish:
- The nature and distribution of the problem.
- Effects on health, livelihoods, income and household security.
- Who has the least ability to avoid exposure or recover from its effects.
- What organisations and public institutions already do.
- Which interventions have credible evidence.
- Where infrastructure could help.
- Where infrastructure would be ineffective without changes in services,
  incentives, enforcement or working arrangements.
- What philanthropy could usefully fund.
- Where further evidence is needed before committing substantial funding.

This is a research assignment. The website build is specified separately in site-brief.md.
Do not stop at a research plan.

1. START WITH DECISIONS, NOT A PRESELECTED SOLUTION

Organise the study around five decisions:

A. Which communities and exposure settings merit priority?
B. Which pathways create the greatest avoidable health and livelihood burdens?
C. Which interventions are effective, usable and institutionally feasible?
D. What should be funded now, tested first or investigated further?
E. What should not be funded on current evidence?

Do not assume the answer is air purifiers, sensors, awareness campaigns,
clean-air shelters or any other particular technology.

Distinguish:
- Reducing emissions.
- Reducing exposure while emissions persist.
- Reducing health consequences.
- Protecting income and care during illness or restrictions.

Explain when these approaches complement one another and when one
cannot substitute for another.

2. DEFINE NATIONAL SCOPE WITH DELHI AS THE ANCHOR

Retain a detailed Delhi/NCR case, but investigate other Indian settings.

Use a purposive comparison rather than an exhaustive city catalogue.
Select approximately five to seven contrasting settings after an
initial evidence scan.

Potential settings to investigate include:
- Delhi/NCR: urban and regional pollution, outdoor work and restrictions.
- Another Indo-Gangetic Plain city or district: persistent regional
  exposure and different institutional capacity.
- An industrial or mining belt: occupational, community and ambient exposure.
- A western or southern metropolitan area: transport, construction,
  industry and informal employment.
- A smaller city with limited monitoring and service capacity.
- A rural setting with household energy and agricultural exposures.
- A peri-urban production cluster, such as kilns or small manufacturing.

These are selection categories, not assumptions about identical conditions.

For each selected setting explain:
- Why it was chosen.
- Pollution and exposure profile.
- Relevant livelihoods and communities.
- Available evidence.
- What it illustrates that Delhi does not.
- What cannot be generalised from it.

Distinguish cities, districts, states, airsheds and occupational settings.
Do not transfer one locality’s source shares or findings to another.

State the research cutoff date.
Separate current conditions, seasonal episodes and long-term exposure.
Use comparable time periods and identify incomplete data.

3. USE “COMMUNITIES OF FATE” AS AN EQUITY LENS

Understand communities of fate as people connected through shared air,
workplaces, neighbourhoods, services and institutions.

Do not treat this as a formal epidemiological category.

Investigate unequal:
- Exposure.
- Biological susceptibility.
- Ability to avoid exposure.
- Access to healthcare.
- Ability to absorb lost earnings and additional costs.
- Voice in decisions.
- Access to public protection and compensation.

Prioritise:
- Street and market vendors.
- Waste pickers and sanitation workers.
- Construction and casual labourers.
- Drivers, delivery workers and other roadside workers.
- Small manufacturing, kiln and industrial workers.
- Home-based workers.
- Agricultural workers where relevant.
- Residents of informal settlements.
- People experiencing homelessness.
- Migrant workers and families.
- Older people.
- Pregnant women.
- Children.
- People with disabilities or chronic illness.

Distinguish self-employment, wage work, piecework, unpaid family work
and employer-employee relationships.

Examine gender, caste, class, migration status, age, disability and
housing tenure where evidence supports their relevance.

Recognise community organisation and knowledge, not only vulnerability.

4. EXPLAIN POLLUTION AND EXPOSURE ACCURATELY

Explain Indian AQI, pollutant concentrations, exposure duration and
personal exposure without conflating them.

Cover relevant particulate and gaseous pollutants.
Distinguish ambient, household and occupational pollution.
Explain how these exposures can overlap without simply adding
incompatible estimates.

Separate:
- Emissions inventories.
- Ambient source apportionment.
- Personal exposure.
- Health burden.
- Economic burden.

Use CPCB documentation for Indian AQI methodology.
Keep units and averaging periods explicit.
Distinguish Indian standards from WHO guidelines.

Compare source-apportionment studies by location, season, date and method.
Do not reduce the national crisis to one source or community.

Explain monitoring gaps and uncertainty.
An unmonitored settlement must not be treated as a low-exposure settlement.

5. MAKE LIVELIHOODS AND INCOME A CENTRAL WORKSTREAM

Investigate five distinct economic pathways:

A. EFFECTS OF EXPOSURE AND ILLNESS
Missed work, reduced working capacity, treatment costs, travel for care,
caregiver time and interruptions to business.

B. EFFECTS OF AVOIDANCE
Shorter working hours, relocation, protective equipment, filtration,
transport changes and other costs of reducing exposure.

C. EFFECTS OF POLLUTION-CONTROL MEASURES
Work stoppages, construction restrictions, transport restrictions,
school closures and compliance costs.

D. LONGER-TERM HOUSEHOLD CONSEQUENCES
Debt, depleted savings, asset sales, interrupted education and reduced
ability to invest in health or livelihoods.

E. DISTRIBUTION OF GAINS AND COSTS
Who gains from cleaner production, who pays for transition, and whether
workers or small enterprises bear disproportionate adjustment costs.

Separate these pathways analytically. Do not attribute all earnings
loss during a pollution episode directly to physiological effects.

Investigate:
- Daily wages and piece rates.
- Informal self-employment revenue and net earnings.
- Seasonal income.
- Paid versus unpaid absence.
- Household consumption and expenditure.
- Debt and coping.
- Women’s unpaid care.
- Small-enterprise margins and compliance capacity.

Do not equate productivity, output, wages, revenue and household income.

For every numerical estimate state:
Population | place | period | pollutant/exposure measure |
economic outcome | method | estimate | uncertainty | limitations.

Distinguish measured losses, self-reported losses, modelled estimates
and hypothetical scenarios.

Do not use national GDP-loss estimates as substitutes for evidence
about a street vendor’s or household’s finances.

6. ASSESS HEALTH, PREGNANCY AND CHILDHOOD

Review established and emerging evidence separately.

Cover relevant respiratory and cardiovascular outcomes, healthcare
use, mortality and other outcomes where credible evidence exists.

Give pregnancy and children dedicated attention:
- Maternal exposure and adverse birth outcomes.
- Relevant exposure windows.
- Children’s respiratory health and lung development.
- School attendance and learning disruption.
- Neurodevelopmental findings, with appropriate caution.
- Access to antenatal, child-health and nutrition services.
- Caregiver time and household earnings.

Distinguish:
- Global evidence.
- Indian evidence.
- Local evidence for the selected setting.
- Biological plausibility.
- Association.
- Strong causal inference.

Do not imply that all outcomes have equal evidence strength.

Explain confounding, exposure measurement and uncertainty.
Do not infer individual causation from population studies.

Treat modelled attributable deaths and life-expectancy losses as
estimates with specified assumptions and counterfactuals.

7. TRACE SECONDARY AND TERTIARY CONSEQUENCES

Develop evidence-labelled pathways such as:

Exposure → illness → missed work → lost earnings → borrowing.

Child illness → additional care → caregiver work interruption →
household financial pressure.

Pollution-control restrictions → work interruption → income loss →
reduced ability to afford protection.

School closure → unequal alternatives → disrupted learning and care.

For every link identify:
- Supporting evidence.
- Geography and population.
- Timescale.
- Alternative explanations.
- Confidence.
- Possible intervention point.

Do not label a whole pathway “proven” because one link is established.

Distinguish health-mediated, behaviour-mediated, market-mediated
and policy-mediated consequences.

Avoid double-counting costs across pathways.

8. BUILD AN EVIDENCE MAP THAT EXPOSES WHAT IS THIN

Assess evidence along two separate dimensions:

A. Confidence that the problem or causal relationship exists.
B. Confidence that the proposed intervention improves outcomes.

Also assess:
- Relevance to vulnerable communities.
- Relevance to India and the selected setting.
- Implementation feasibility.
- Equity of access.
- Costs and long-term operation.

Use transparent categories:
Strong | moderate | limited/mixed | no adequate evidence found.

Explain each judgement rather than assigning unexplained scores.

Distinguish:
- Absence of evidence.
- Evidence of no effect.
- Evidence of harm.
- Evidence of benefit under conditions that may not transfer locally.

Highlight under-studied groups and outcomes, especially informal
earnings, unpaid care, pregnancy, small-enterprise effects and
long-term maintenance.

Do not classify an intervention as ineffective merely because it
has not been studied locally.

9. MAP ORGANISATIONS, PROGRAMMES AND RESPONSIBILITIES

Research:
- Public authorities and municipal bodies.
- Pollution-control and public-health institutions.
- Academic and research organisations.
- Worker and vendor organisations.
- Community and women’s organisations.
- NGOs and implementation organisations.
- Philanthropic funders and intermediaries.
- Relevant enterprises and infrastructure operators.

Candidate research leads include CPCB, state pollution-control boards,
CAQM where applicable, municipal departments, health institutions,
CSE, CEEW, TERI, relevant IITs, worker organisations and locally active NGOs.

Verify actual activities rather than assuming involvement.

For each record:
Organisation | programme | location | communities served |
specific activity | current status | partners | funding where public |
outputs | demonstrated outcomes | evidence quality | gaps | source.

Separate advocacy, research, monitoring, delivery, financing and enforcement.

Identify:
- Established work that could be strengthened.
- Duplication.
- Places without delivery capacity.
- Gaps between research and implementation.
- Missing worker/community participation.
- Institutions responsible for recurring services.

Use public organisational contact routes only.
Do not contact anyone or infer private relationships.

10. EXAMINE INFRASTRUCTURE AS A SPECIFIC INVESTMENT QUESTION

Investigate potential infrastructure categories without endorsing them
in advance:

A. EMISSION-REDUCING INFRASTRUCTURE
Clean energy and reliable fuel access; appropriate industrial upgrades;
waste collection and processing; transport and freight improvements;
relevant construction and dust-control systems.

B. EXPOSURE-REDUCING INFRASTRUCTURE
Building improvements and suitable filtration in schools, childcare,
clinics, community facilities and workplaces; cleaner-air spaces;
appropriate local exhaust or source capture; housing improvements.

C. HEALTH AND SERVICE INFRASTRUCTURE
Primary-care readiness, referral arrangements, outreach capability
and continuity of essential services during severe episodes.

D. INFORMATION AND ACCOUNTABILITY INFRASTRUCTURE
Monitoring, exposure assessment, public data and decision systems
where a clear operational response follows the information.

Do not call every activity “infrastructure”.
Distinguish physical assets, equipment, operating services and institutions.

FOR EACH CANDIDATE ANSWER:

- What specific pollutant or pathway does it address?
- Does it reduce emissions, exposure or consequences?
- Who uses it, and for how many hours?
- What measured performance supports it?
- Is there evidence of health or livelihood benefit?
- What permissions and technical design are required?
- Who owns, operates and maintains it?
- Who pays for electricity, consumables, staff and replacement?
- What happens after the grant ends?
- Are tenants, migrants and informal workers able to access it?
- Could user fees exclude the intended beneficiaries?
- Could pollution be displaced elsewhere?
- Are there heat, ventilation, fire, occupational-safety or other trade-offs?
- Is a simpler service or policy change more effective?
- What would justify stopping or redesigning the investment?

For filtration, assess room conditions, clean-air delivery, occupancy,
outdoor infiltration, maintenance and time actually spent in the space.
Do not assume reductions in particles imply control of gases.

For cleaner-air shelters, investigate whether intended users can
practically attend without losing earnings, care or access to services.

For sensors, identify the decision, responsible actor and response budget.
Monitoring alone is not an exposure-reduction outcome.

For mechanisation or cleaner production, assess livelihood transitions
and who bears capital and compliance costs.

Assess high-profile technologies against independent evidence.
Do not recommend infrastructure because it is visible or easily branded.

11. COMPARE INFRASTRUCTURE WITH OTHER INTERVENTIONS

Assess complementary measures:
- Emission regulation and implementation capacity.
- Cleaner production support.
- Worker protections and paid arrangements.
- Income continuity during restrictions.
- Affordable healthcare and referral.
- School and care-service continuity.
- Community organisation and accountability.
- Practical risk communication.
- Legal and administrative access to existing entitlements.

For each explain what philanthropy can influence and what depends
on government, employers or other duty-bearers.

Do not frame grants as a permanent replacement for statutory services
or employer obligations.

Separate recommendations that can proceed within existing institutions
from those requiring policy or regulatory change.

12. DEVELOP A GRANTMAKING THESIS AND ALTERNATIVES

Produce three or four distinct portfolio options grounded in findings.

For each:
- Problem and priority population.
- Evidence supporting action.
- Proposed intervention combination.
- Why philanthropy is needed.
- Additionality relative to existing programmes.
- Potential partners.
- Geography and selection rationale.
- Infrastructure component, if justified.
- Recurring-service requirements.
- Expected outcomes and credible measurement.
- Main uncertainties and failure modes.
- Sustainability and institutional adoption.
- Conditions for expansion, redesign or exit.

Distinguish:
A. Ready to support based on credible evidence.
B. Promising but needs adaptation and implementation learning.
C. Requires proof of concept or stronger causal evidence.
D. Not recommended on present evidence.

Do not assume a single flagship project is superior to a portfolio.
Compare place-based and population-based approaches.

For example, investigate whether a portfolio around vendors and
informal markets would create different benefits and delivery challenges
from one around schools, maternal care or industrial communities.
Treat these as alternatives to assess, not predetermined recommendations.

Do not invent costs, returns or cost-effectiveness ratios.
Use sourced ranges or explicitly labelled planning assumptions.
Separate capital expenditure, operating expenditure and evaluation costs.

13. PROPOSE A PROPORTIONATE LEARNING AGENDA

Identify which uncertainties can be resolved through:
- Better use of existing evidence.
- Exposure and service assessments.
- Qualitative research with affected communities.
- Feasibility studies.
- Implementation pilots.
- Quasi-experimental evaluation.
- Randomised studies where ethical and appropriate.

Do not demand a randomised trial for every intervention.
Do not delay established protective measures solely to create a study.

Specify outcomes across:
Exposure | health | earnings | out-of-pocket costs |
care burden | uptake | access | institutional performance.

Explain when health outcomes need longer follow-up.
Use exposure measures as intermediate outcomes without pretending
they establish a particular health benefit.

Include distributional analysis: who benefits, who does not,
and whether any group bears additional costs.

Any future human-subject research must have appropriate consent,
privacy protection and ethics review. This assignment is desk research.

14. SOURCE AND RESEARCH RULES

Prioritise official datasets, primary research, systematic reviews,
credible evaluations and documented community research.

Use official programme documents to establish policy design.
Use implementation evidence to establish whether the policy reaches people.

Use organisational reporting cautiously for claimed outcomes.
Label journalism, testimony and programme descriptions appropriately.

Search relevant Indian and international literature.
Include Hindi and other relevant local-language material where useful.

Preserve original source links and publication dates.
Maintain a claim-level evidence register.

Do not invent:
- Current programmes.
- Quantitative impacts.
- Community quotations.
- Costs.
- Partnerships.
- Funding relationships.
- Claims of programme success.

If evidence is inaccessible or contradictory, record that clearly.
If browsing is unavailable, identify the work as incomplete.

15. REQUIRED OUTPUTS

A. DECISION BRIEF: 3–4 pages
- Core judgement.
- Why this matters for vulnerable communities.
- Most consequential livelihood pathways.
- Geographic differences.
- Strongest intervention opportunities.
- Infrastructure judgement.
- Evidence gaps.
- Two or three decisions a foundation must make.

B. FULL RESEARCH REPORT
Approximately 7,000–10,000 words excluding appendices.
Use progressive depth and avoid repeating the same findings.

C. GEOGRAPHIC COMPARISON TABLE
Setting | pollution/exposure pattern | communities |
health and livelihood evidence | existing responses | gaps.

D. COMMUNITY IMPACT MATRIX
Group | exposure | direct impacts | secondary/tertiary impacts |
protective constraints | evidence confidence.

E. ORGANISATION AND PROGRAMME MAP
Verified activities, responsibilities, reach and outcome evidence.

F. INTERVENTION AND INFRASTRUCTURE MATRIX
Intervention | evidence | equity | capital/recurring needs |
operator | institutional dependencies | risks |
fund now/test first/not recommended.

G. EVIDENCE-GAP REGISTER
Question | what is known | what is missing |
why it matters to an investment decision | proportionate next study.

H. THREE OR FOUR PORTFOLIO CONCEPTS
Approximately one page each, including alternatives and failure conditions.

I. SOURCE REGISTER AND REPRODUCIBLE ANALYSIS
Include datasets, calculation methods and limitations where analysis is performed.

Save the report in Markdown and structured tables in CSV.
If suitable tools are available, create a clean, readable PDF.
Use clear filenames and include the research cutoff.

QUALITY CHECK BEFORE FINISHING

Verify:
- Delhi remains a detailed case without becoming a proxy for all India.
- Health, productivity, earnings and household welfare are distinguished.
- Every causal claim matches the underlying evidence.
- Policy-related losses are separated from pollution-related illness.
- No monitoring gap is interpreted as absence of risk.
- Infrastructure recommendations include an operator and recurring finance.
- Equity is assessed through access and outcomes, not just stated intent.
- Existing organisations and obligations are recognised.
- Recommendations can be traced back to findings.
- The report identifies what not to fund and why.

The final synthesis should answer:

“Where can a foundation help reduce the pollution people face,
protect the livelihoods they depend on, and leave behind a service
or institution that continues to work after the grant ends?”
